# HEADLINE FORMAT — the structure the card headlines use

Learned 25 October 2026 from the first card made in-house: *Founder's 10X Scale Question*.

> **The quietest moment on set isn't a forgotten script, it's when a founder is asked
> WHAT BREAKS AT 10X SCALE.**

That is the reference. Everything below is that sentence taken apart.

---

## 1. The five slots

```
The [SUPERLATIVE] [moment/thing] on [PLACE]
    isn't [THE OBVIOUS ANSWER],
    it's when [A SPECIFIC HUMAN MOMENT]
    [THE PAYOFF, IN CAPS, WITH A NUMBER IN IT].
```

| Slot | In the reference | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Superlative + place | "The quietest moment on set" | a superlative pointed at something nobody calls superlative. Quiet, not loud. That inversion is the hook |
| 2. The obvious answer, rejected | "isn't a forgotten script" | names what the reader expected, then kills it. The reader feels corrected, which is what makes them keep reading |
| 3. The pivot | "it's when…" | turns the sentence. Always "it's when", never "it is because" |
| 4. A specific human moment | "a founder is asked" | passive voice on purpose. The founder is the subject of the moment, not the speaker |
| 5. The payoff in caps | "WHAT BREAKS AT 10X SCALE." | the only capitalised words on the card. The screenshot fragment. Contains a number |

**Word count: 18–24.** The reference is 22. Long enough to carry an argument, short enough
to sit on three lines over a photograph.

---

## 2. The rules

1. **Exactly one caps run, and it goes last.** Caps is the visual anchor. Two caps runs and
   the card has no anchor, only shouting.
2. **The caps line carries the number.** 10X, 30, 2 AM, ₹10 crore. A payoff without a
   number is an opinion; a payoff with one is a claim.
3. **The negation must name a real cliché.** "a forgotten script", "the deck", "the market
   size". If the thing being rejected is not something the reader actually expected, the
   turn lands on nothing.
4. **The place must be two or three words.** "on set", "at that table", "in that room".
   A long place description eats the line.
5. **No adjectives in the payoff.** "WHAT BREAKS AT 10X SCALE" has none. The moment
   adjectives enter the caps, the line stops sounding spoken.
6. **It must be sayable in one breath.** Read it aloud. If you run out of air before the
   caps, it is a paragraph, not a headline.
7. **The description answers the headline, it does not repeat it.** The headline names the
   moment; the body delivers it. Never open the body by restating the caps line.

## 3b. The variant: caps on the term, not the payoff

The second card made in-house uses the same skeleton and moves the caps run:

> **Patents and innovation get you published.
> DISTRIBUTION DENSITY determines if you survive.**

Two changes, both deliberate and both fine:

1. **Two sentences instead of one comma join.** The first states the thing that gets you
   *published*; the second states the thing that decides whether you *survive*. The contrast
   is the whole headline, so it gets its own sentence.
2. **The caps run sits mid-sentence and marks a coined term**, not the closing payoff.

The rule that reconciles both cards: **caps go on the term you are claiming.** In the 10X
card the claim is a question the founder is asked, so the caps land at the end. In this card
the claim is a coinage — "distribution density" — so the caps land on the coinage and the
sentence continues past it.

What does not change: **one caps run only, and it is always the thing you would screenshot.**
The caps never mark a whole sentence, and never mark two different things.

A coined term in caps has one extra obligation: **the description has to make the term mean
something.** A caps coinage the body never defines is a slogan. The 10X card's caps line is
self-explanatory; this one is not, so the body owes the reader a working definition.

---

## 3c. The variant with no caps at all: the ellipsis ending

The third card drops the caps run entirely and ends on an ellipsis:

> **A selection round is not a verdict.
> It is one person defending a founder while another tests the defence…**

Here the emphasis is carried by the highlight bars, and the ellipsis does the job the caps
were doing — it marks the phrase as the thing to hold on to. The difference is what it asks
of the caption: **a caps line closes the thought, an ellipsis hands it over.** The description
then has to complete it rather than answer it.

Three cards, three ways to mark the claim:

| Card | Emphasis device | What the caption owes |
|---|---|---|
| 10X Scale Question | caps on the closing payoff | answer the question the caps poses |
| Distribution Density | caps on a coined term, mid-sentence | define the coinage |
| Defending Founders | ellipsis, no caps | finish the sentence |

The constant across all three: **exactly one emphasis device per card, and it sits on the
thing you would screenshot.** Two devices and the card has no anchor.

## 4. What breaks the format

| Do not | Why |
|---|---|
| "Here's what I learned about scaling from 5,000 founders" | no negation, no turn, no caps. It is a blog title, not a card |
| "The quietest moment on set isn't a forgotten script. It's when a founder is asked what breaks at 10x scale." | no caps, so the card has no visual anchor; also 25 words once the full stop lands |
| Two caps runs, e.g. "NOBODY REHEARSES THE PAUSE. IT'S THE 90 SECONDS AFTER THE PITCH." | no anchor, all shout |
| A question mark at the end | the caps payoff is question-*shaped*, never punctuated as a question. The card makes a statement |
| "WHAT BREAKS AT 10X SCALE?" with a question mark | same reason, and it hands the reader an out — they can answer "nothing" and scroll |
| Hype in the caps ("THE BRUTAL TRUTH ABOUT SCALE") | house voice rule: no hype, no adjectives doing the work a number should do |

---

## 5. One thing to know about the reference card

**"What breaks at 10x" is his own line.** He published it: *"What breaks when this becomes
10x bigger? And what does the founder understand that the rest of us don't yet?"*

The card's version keeps the shape and changes the words, which is the right way to reuse it.
But it is the second time the idea goes out, so treat it as a **signature question** — fine to
return to deliberately, never fine to run as a fresh insight. If it comes back a third time,
it needs new material under it, not a new coat of paint.
