## FINAL POST (v7 — LinkedIn)

> In the beginning, the founder is the answer to every unfinished question.
>
> That is not a flaw. Early on, the company has no muscle memory of its own, so you become it. I approved the spend, settled the disagreement, decided what came next, and for a while that is exactly why we moved faster than anyone else.
>
> Then the same instinct becomes the ceiling. Every decision still routes back to me, and the company starts moving at the speed of one person's calendar.
>
> Which of them still need me, and which only feel like they do?
>
> Delegating harder rarely fixes this. The real job is telling the two apart, then letting the rest run without me.

## HISTORY — v5 (superseded, kept for reference)

> I have now sat on both sides of the table. Building companies taught me one thing, and
> judging them taught me another, and those two lessons do not always agree.
>
> A polished pitch only tells you the founder can present. It says nothing about whether the
> business will hold. The reverse is also true, and it is the part people forget: the most
> serious builder in the room is sometimes the one with the average deck.
>
> So I stopped scoring the story and started listening for the question underneath it. What
> does this person understand that nobody else in the room has noticed yet, and will it still
> hold when the company is ten times bigger?
>
> That question is usually where a real company begins, and it almost never fits neatly on a
> slide.
