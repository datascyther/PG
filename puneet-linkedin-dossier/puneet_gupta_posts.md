Recently, I collaborated with Parineeti Chopra and have onboarded her as a Partner and Investor of Clensta which went viral and brought much-needed attention. 

Well, I understand such moments are important for business growth but at the same time, they come with a lot of responsibility. 

The to-do list seems never-ending, and it is easy to get overwhelmed when juggling a number of initiatives and every task seems equally important. 

Now as an entrepreneur, I have learned the value of strategy and prioritization. I use the ABC Analysis Method to strike a balance between completing existing tasks and initiating new ones. 

Here's how it works, Priority A tasks are the ones that demand immediate attention. Think of them as the "tyres" for your business, without them, things can not move forward smoothly. 

In Priority B,  tasks are important but do not require immediate action. They fall into the mid-term category.

Priority C forms part of your long-term vision. While they may not need immediate attention, they should not be forgotten.

By categorizing tasks this way, I gain clarity on what needs your immediate attention also I ensure my BAU is maintained while allowing space for strategic, long-term initiatives.

I was wrong. And I need to say this out loud.
20 years ago, I was selected to serve in the Indian Defence.
And even today, I carry that mindset — move fast, adapt under fire, survive first, solve later.

But that mindset doesn’t always work in business.

I used to believe in the idea: “Jab nadi aayegi tab pul banayenge.”
That we’ll figure it out when the problem comes.

But I was wrong.
If you don’t plan ahead, you don’t get a second chance — the chaos, the pressure, and the emergencies will drown you.

You don’t build the bridge during the flood.
You build it before.

I also used to believe that if people got to learn and earn, they’d stay.
Later, I added “recognition” to that list — a belief I called LSD:
Lakshmi (wealth), Saraswati (knowledge), and Durga (recognition).

But I was wrong again.

More than learning, earning, or even recognition — great companies are built on processes.
Not passion. Not charisma. Not just product.
If the engine doesn’t work without the founder, it’s not an engine. It’s a hustle.

From aerospace at Honeywell,
to building optoelectronics for the armed forces,
to inventing waterless tech with IIT Delhi,
to building a consumer brand from scratch —

Every step was powered by resilience, naivety, and a bit of madness.

We turned Clensta from an idea to an international brand.
We scaled Clensta to a point where it was valued at $36 million, and it has now been acquired.
Cumulatively, I’ve built startups worth over $100 million.
What I built taught me a lot. What I lost taught me more.

I’m deeply thankful to every investor, customer, and team member who believed, built, and stayed through the highs and lows.
Alongside that gratitude, there are also hard truths we rarely speak aloud.
But here’s what they don’t tell you in the headlines:

Not everything goes right.
Some decisions hurt people.
Some debts remain.
Some lessons are expensive.

One of my board members once told me:
“Puneet, it will never be smooth sailing.”

He was right. And what they don’t tell you is — sometimes the storm comes from inside the ship.

“The founder must be the last to leave. You take the hit. You hold the line. You leave the light on.”

I’m stepping down as CEO.

Not in retreat.
But in reflection.

Back to zero.
Back to stealth.

I will share more soon — about the road, the rebuild, and what I’ve learned along the way. In the background, I’m deepening my lens on AI and emerging tech — sharpening tools for what’s next.
But for now — I’m taking time to reflect and reset.

The customer is never wrong. Your assumptions usually are.

One habit has stayed with me throughout my entrepreneurial journey: I spend far more time with buyers than with presentations.

Some time ago, during a walk with Sudhir Rajpal IAS, Additional Chief Secretary, Government of Haryana, our conversation drifted towards how difficult decisions are made when they affect millions of people.

One insight stayed with me.

The most experienced decision-makers don’t start with solutions. They start by understanding the problem from the ground up.

It reminded me of the biggest mistake founders make. We become emotionally attached to what we want to build, instead of obsessing over what the customer actually needs.

Customers don’t reward innovation.

They reward relevance.

The best products are rarely built by the smartest people in the room. They are built by the people who spend the most time listening.

I started learning golf about a year ago, and it has taught me more than I expected.

You can learn more about a person in one round of golf than in ten meetings with them. Everyone assumes the course is where deals get made. That part is true, but it is not the real reason people keep going back to it. Golf is where character quietly shows itself.

There is no referee in this game. Your ball lands in the rough, in a bad spot, and nobody is watching you. You can nudge it a couple of inches to an easier lie and no one would ever know. Or you can play it where it sits and take the harder shot. That small moment, seen by no one, tells you everything you need to know about a person. Whether they will cut a corner when honesty costs them nothing more than a slightly worse score.

That is why the seasoned players say the course reveals who you are. It is four quiet hours of watching what someone does when the rules are theirs to bend and the truth is theirs to report.

Founder and investor relationships run on exactly the same thing. The contract handles the parts everyone can see. Everything that actually matters happens in the moments no one is watching.

In the end you are never really betting on the pitch. You are betting on how someone plays when they are certain no one is looking.

The best partnerships are simply two people who both refuse to nudge the ball.
