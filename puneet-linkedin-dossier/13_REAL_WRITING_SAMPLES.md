### Sample 12 — Golf / nudge the ball

I started learning golf about a year ago, and it has taught me more than I expected.

You can learn more about a person in one round of golf than in ten meetings with them. Everyone assumes the course is where deals get made. That part is true, but it is not the real reason people keep going back to it. Golf is where character quietly shows itself.

There is no referee in this game. Your ball lands in the rough, in a bad spot, and nobody is watching you. You can nudge it a couple of inches to an easier lie and no one would ever know. Or you can play it where it sits and take the harder shot. That small moment, seen by no one, tells you everything you need to know about a person. Whether they will cut a corner when honesty costs them nothing more than a slightly worse score.

That is why the seasoned players say the course reveals who you are. It is four quiet hours of watching what someone does when the rules are theirs to bend and the truth is theirs to report.

Founder and investor relationships run on exactly the same thing. The contract handles the parts everyone can see. Everything that actually matters happens in the moments no one is watching.

In the end you are never really betting on the pitch. You are betting on how someone plays when they are certain no one is looking.

The best partnerships are simply two people who both refuse to nudge the ball.

### Sample 20 — Every mountain is not worth climbing

Every mountain is not worth climbing.

I think founders make the opposite mistake. We chase the biggest market, the loudest trend, or the highest valuation. But the right mission almost always beats the biggest opportunity.

### Sample 24 — Packing up the old office / status

I was packing up my old office last year and it took me two hours to take one wall down.

That wall had every award, certificate and framed photo from ten years of work.

I took them off one by one, wrapped them, put them in a carton. Somewhere in the middle of it I noticed something. The most recent one was years old. Everything I had put up there, I had stopped adding to.

I sat on the floor with that carton for a while. There are two games you can play. One is building, where nobody has to lose for you to win. The other is ranking, where somebody does. That wall was a ranking. I had spent a long time being proud of it and no time noticing it had stopped moving.

I am not going to say status doesn't matter. In India it is sometimes the only capital a first-time founder has. It gets you the meeting, it makes the cold call warm, it opens a room nobody invited you into. The problem was never having it. The problem is holding it. Status you spend becomes leverage. Status you display becomes your identity, and once it is your identity you stop taking risks, because a risk can make you look smaller than the wall says you are.

The carton is still unopened in the new office. I have not felt the need to put any of it back up.

### Sample 25 — Faith Before Proof (Pushkar)

Faith Before Proof

Over the weekend, I found some quiet time in Pushkar. While walking around the temples and sitting by the lake, I struck up a conversation with an elderly pujari. He smiled and asked, "Do you remember why this place is called Pushkar?" I had almost forgotten.

He reminded me of the old legend. Lord Brahma dropped a lotus from his hand, and where its petals fell, Pushkar Lake came into existence. Pushpa (flower) and Kar (hand). I had heard the story years ago, but hearing it there felt completely different.

On the drive back, one thought stayed with me. For centuries, people have travelled here because of faith. They didn't come because someone showed them proof; they came because they believed.

Maybe entrepreneurship begins the same way. Every enduring company starts with one person who has the courage to believe before anyone else does.

Before customers, investors, or the market have faith in your vision, you have to carry it yourself. Happy Monday.

### Sample 26 — Customer assumptions / Sudhir Rajpal IAS

The customer is never wrong. Your assumptions usually are.

One habit has stayed with me throughout my entrepreneurial journey: I spend far more time with buyers than with presentations.

Some time ago, during a walk with Sudhir Rajpal IAS, Additional Chief Secretary, Government of Haryana, our conversation drifted towards how difficult decisions are made when they affect millions of people.

One insight stayed with me.

The most experienced decision-makers don't start with solutions. They start by understanding the problem from the ground up.

It reminded me of the biggest mistake founders make. We become emotionally attached to what we want to build, instead of obsessing over what the customer actually needs.

Customers don't reward innovation.

They reward relevance.

The best products are rarely built by the smartest people in the room. They are built by the people who spend the most time listening.
