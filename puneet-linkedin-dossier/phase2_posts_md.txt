Reflecting upon my journey with Clensta over the years, I have had the privilege of working alongside a diverse and talented team. 

This experience has highlighted the importance of effective teamwork. 

As someone who has also been part of previous ventures, I have observed a recurring phenomenon that I would like to share with you today.

I have noticed that within team structures, there's sometimes a tendency to follow instructions of senior leaders and this reminds me of a 1961 theory suggested by psychologist Stanley Milgram referred to as Milgram Obedience.  

This study conducted at Yale University delves into the extent to which people are willing to obey authority figures. 

I somewhat disagree with this theory because I feel senior leadership at times knows how to drive the situation given the experiences they have had in their career. 

At Clensta, I appreciate that every team member brings a unique set of skills and insights to the table. 

Rather than subscribing to a one-size-fits-all approach, I feel it is about proactive engagement, critical thinking, and the application of our individual strengths.

“Can we build something together?”

Every time I look at my DBA results from Golden Gate University, I laugh a little.

GenAI Pre-trained Models: 96%
Case Study: 96%
Overall CGPA: 3.76/4

I am beginning to suspect either:

My professors are extremely kind, or
AI has finally helped me discover whatever IQ was hiding all these years.

For founders, numbers usually come from CAC, LTV, burn, and ARR.
This time the numbers are coming from academic evaluation sheets.
Surprising… but meaningful.

Because something interesting is happening in the world.
The companies that will define the next decade will not be “built on AI.”
They will be “built through AI” — systems where intelligence, risk, behaviour, and incentives interact in real time.

To build for that world, intuition is not enough.
EQ tells you why humans behave the way they do.
IQ (and a little bit of GPU time) tells you how to model it.
The magic is always in the overlap.

This is why I am going deep into the technical foundation again.
Not for the certificate.
Not for a pivot.
But because the next generation of problems will need founders who can speak both languages — the emotional vocabulary of humans and the mathematical vocabulary of machines.

And frankly, it is fun to surprise yourself.

Learning continues.
Curiosity compounds.
And sometimes, numbers do tell a story — even when they are on a report card.

I started learning golf about a year ago, and it has taught me more than I expected.

You can learn more about a person in one round of golf than in ten meetings with them. Everyone assumes the course is where deals get made. That part is true, but it is not the real reason people keep going back to it. Golf is where character quietly shows itself.

There is no referee in this game. Your ball lands in the rough, in a bad spot, and nobody is watching you. You can nudge it a couple of inches to an easier lie and no one would ever know. Or you can play it where it sits and take the harder shot. That small moment, seen by no one, tells you everything you need to know about a person. Whether they will cut a corner when honesty costs them nothing more than a slightly worse score.

That is why the seasoned players say the course reveals who you are. It is four quiet hours of watching what someone does when the rules are theirs to bend and the truth is theirs to report.

Founder and investor relationships run on exactly the same thing. The contract handles the parts everyone can see. Everything that actually matters happens in the moments no one is watching.

In the end you are never really betting on the pitch. You are betting on how someone plays when they are certain no one is looking.

The best partnerships are simply two people who both refuse to nudge the ball.
